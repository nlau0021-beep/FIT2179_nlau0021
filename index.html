Data Insight Report

Living Longer, Ageing Faster

Malaysia's quiet demographic transition and what it means for our health.

The hook: we're living longer than ever

In a generation, Malaysia has nearly doubled in size — from around 18 million people in 1991 to 35 million today. But the story isn't just about growth. It's about who we are, how long we live, and the quiet pressure that longer lives place on a health system built for a younger country.

✦ ✦ ✦

Where the ageing is happening

The pressure of an ageing population isn't evenly spread. The peninsular west coast — Perak, Penang, Selangor, Kuala Lumpur — has aged the fastest. East Malaysia (Sabah, Sarawak) remains the youngest part of the country.

The shape of the population

Each bar shows how many people are in a 5-year age band. Read it like a tree ring: the bands at the bottom (children) versus those above (older cohorts) tell you how the population is changing over time.

Same country, four different demographic shapes

Zoom in on individual states and the picture becomes more varied. Sabah still looks like a textbook population pyramid — wide at the base, narrowing upward. Perak's pyramid has lost its bottom: fewer children entering each year. Kuala Lumpur bulges in the middle, dominated by working-age migrants drawn to the capital.

✦ ✦ ✦

What an older Malaysia means for our health

As the population ages, the diseases that kill us shift. Today, eight of Malaysia's top ten causes of death are non-communicable — chronic illnesses that build up over decades of life.

The 65% problem

Put another way: out of every 100 Malaysians who die today, 65 die from a chronic disease. These are deaths that, in most cases, could have been prevented or delayed with earlier intervention.

That share has grown steadily over the past two decades, and is set to keep growing as the population ages.

How does Malaysia compare regionally?

The probability that a 30-year-old will die from a major chronic disease before age 70 is a single, powerful measure. Malaysia sits in the middle of the ASEAN pack — making slower progress than Singapore or Thailand, but better than Indonesia or the Philippines.

✦ ✦ ✦

Watch the ageing wave spread

The same map, three different decades. The colour intensity shows how much of each state's population is aged 65 or older. In 1991, only Penang and Perak had a visibly older population. By 2025, every state has aged.

The pressure ahead

Behind every retiree are the working-age adults who pay the taxes, fill the jobs, and provide the care. Some Malaysian states have plenty of workers per retiree; others are already running thin.

Where each state stands on the demographic clock

Two demographic forces shape a state's future: how old its people are today, and how fast its population is changing. Each state sits in one of four quadrants — and the quadrant tells you what challenges are coming next.
